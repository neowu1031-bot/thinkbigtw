Shared Reading & Custom Picture Books Q16

How to Read Feelings Books: 4 Steps to Help a Child Put Emotions Into Words

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-26   ·  中文

The short answer

When reading emotions picture books, the key is to let the child "talk" after reading: first point to the character's expression and ask "what is this character feeling right now," then ask "why do they feel that way," then ask "if it were you, how would you say it," and finally ask "what can they do to feel better." These 4 steps correspond to the four abilities of the emotions domain in the Ministry of Education's Preschool Curriculum Framework: awareness and identification, understanding, expression, and regulation; the framework also lists characters in stories and videos as one of the objects through which young children practice emotions.

1. What the curriculum framework says about emotional abilities

The Ministry of Education's Preschool Curriculum Framework, promulgated December 1, 2016, lists emotions as one of six domains and divides the ability to handle emotions into four categories. (Ministry of Education Legal Information):

  • Awareness and identification: When an emotion arises, being able to identify what emotion it is.
  • Understanding: Figuring out what happened and what one thinks about it.
  • Expression: Understanding the rules of one's cultural context and expressing emotions at appropriate times, in appropriate situations, and in appropriate roles.
  • Regulation: Using various strategies to change negative or overly intense emotions.

The framework divides learning into two dimensions: "self" and "others and environment." The "environment" in "others and environment" refers to the emotional responses that young children project onto animals, objects, texts and videos in their environment—that is, anthropomorphized characters in stories and videos. In other words, the framework also lists characters in stories and videos as objects through which young children practice emotions.

The framework also notes that both positive and negative emotions have reasons for appearing and are worth exploring; what young children need to learn is to accept their emotions, not to deny them.

2. 4 steps for reading an emotions picture book

StepWhat you can sayCorresponding ability
1. Find the expression"Look at their eyebrows and mouth—what are they feeling right now?"Awareness and identification
2. Say the reason"Why are they angry? What just happened?"Understanding
3. Try a different way to say it"If you were them, how would you tell your friend?"Expression
4. Think of a solution"What can they do to feel better?"Regulation

The ability names and categories come from the curriculum framework; the steps and questions in the left two columns are designed based on these four abilities as a way to do shared reading—they are not the original text of the framework.

The framework notes that young children's learning about emotions typically starts with themselves and gradually expands to others and objects in the environment. So after reading about the character's feelings, you can turn the question back to the child: "When do you feel scared?"

3. Things to keep in mind while reading

Don't rush to teach a lesson. If the child says "they're very angry," first respond "right, they're very angry," rather than immediately adding "being angry is bad." This echoes the framework's direction of "accepting emotions rather than denying them."

Only discuss one or two pages at a time. This is a practical recommendation: pick the page with the most obvious expression, and read normally if the child doesn't want to discuss. For questioning techniques, see How to do dialogic reading.

Shared reading itself is beneficial. The AAP's 2024 policy statement in Pediatrics notes that shared reading strengthens the parent-child relationship and is one of the foundations of children's social-emotional, cognitive, language, and literacy development. (Pediatrics 2024)

FAQ

Q1: How do I read an emotions picture book?
Use 4 steps: find the expression (what are they feeling right now), say the reason (why do they feel that way), try a different way to say it (if it were you, how would you say it), and think of a solution (what can they do to feel better). These 4 steps correspond to the four abilities in the Ministry of Education's Preschool Curriculum Framework emotions domain: awareness and identification, understanding, expression, and regulation.
Q2: What emotional abilities are covered in the preschool curriculum?
The Ministry of Education's Preschool Curriculum Framework divides emotional abilities into four categories: emotional awareness and identification, emotional expression, emotional understanding, and emotional regulation, divided into the two learning dimensions of "self" and "others and environment."
Q3: Does a child need to skip pages with angry or scared content?
There is no special need to skip them. The framework notes that both positive and negative emotions have reasons for appearing and are worth exploring; what young children need to learn is to accept their emotions rather than deny them. When reading such content, you can pause and ask the child why the character feels that way.
