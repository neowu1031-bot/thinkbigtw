How to Read Feelings Books: 4 Steps to Help a Child Put Emotions Into Words

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-26   ·  中文

The short answer

Reading about emotions works best when it goes beyond 'this character is sad.' Taiwan's Ministry of Education Preschool Curriculum Guidelines (issued December 1, 2016) identify four abilities in the emotions domain: awareness and identification, understanding, expression, and regulation. A picture book used as a conversation starting point can support all four. The key is not to skip over negative emotions or tell children how they should feel—the guidelines explicitly state that both positive and negative emotions have causes worth exploring, and that children should learn to accept rather than deny emotions.

1. Taiwan's preschool emotions curriculum framework

The Ministry of Education Preschool Curriculum Guidelines (Revised Edition, December 1, 2016) define emotions as one of six domains of preschool education (alongside physical health and movement; language; cognition; aesthetics; and social). Within the emotions domain, four abilities are targeted:

  • Awareness and identification: Noticing that an emotion exists in self or another.
  • Understanding: Connecting the emotion to its cause ("he is sad because his toy broke").
  • Expression: Finding words, gestures, or actions to communicate the emotion.
  • Regulation: Managing the emotion so it does not overwhelm behavior.

The guidelines' learning dimensions include both "self" and "others and environment"—meaning children should learn to identify emotions in anthropomorphized characters in stories and videos, not only in themselves. The guidelines specifically state that "positive and negative emotions both have causes worth exploring" and that an important outcome is children learning to accept rather than deny emotions.

2. Four steps to reading a feelings book

Step 1: Spot the expression. Point to the character's face and body language. "What does this face look like? What is their body doing?" You are building the awareness-and-identification skill.

Step 2: Explain the cause. "They are feeling that way because ___ happened." If the child does not know, tell them. You are building the understanding skill. Do not skip this step for negative emotions—the curriculum guidelines specifically note that negative emotions have causes worth exploring.

Step 3: Suggest words. Give the specific emotion word: "scared," "frustrated," "left out," "proud." Children who have precise emotional vocabulary can express and regulate better than those with only "happy" and "sad." You are building the expression skill.

Step 4: Propose one coping action. "When I feel that way, I ___ ." Model one action—taking a breath, asking for a hug, squeezing something soft. You are building the regulation skill. Keep it concrete and achievable, not "think positive thoughts."

3. What not to do

  • Don’t say "that’s not how you should feel." The goal is acceptance and expression, not correction.
  • Don’t rush to Step 4. Skipping from "that character is sad" straight to "here is how to fix it" bypasses the most important steps: identifying and understanding the feeling.
  • Don’t only use books when the child is upset. Regular readings build vocabulary; reading only in crisis is too late to build the skill.

FAQ

Q1: What are the four emotional abilities in Taiwan's preschool curriculum?
Taiwan's Ministry of Education Preschool Curriculum Guidelines define four abilities: awareness and identification (noticing the emotion), understanding (connecting it to a cause), expression (finding words or actions for it), and regulation (managing it). Picture books support all four.
Q2: Should I read about negative emotions too?
Yes. Taiwan's Preschool Curriculum Guidelines explicitly state that positive and negative emotions both have causes worth exploring, and that an important outcome is children learning to accept rather than deny emotions. Skipping or minimizing negative emotions in books misses the main developmental purpose.
Q3: How do feelings books help children?
By providing examples outside the child's own emotional state—seeing a character feel something in a book is lower-stakes than feeling it yourself. This gives children vocabulary (knowing the word 'frustrated') and understanding (connecting the emotion to a cause) before they need to apply those skills in real situations.
