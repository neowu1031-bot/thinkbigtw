A New Baby Is Coming: How to Prepare an Older Child, and How Picture Books Can Help

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-16   ·  中文

The short answer

There is no single perfect moment to tell an older child about a new sibling. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends choosing a natural moment, using language the child can relate to in terms they can perceive (such as 'when it gets cold outside'), and avoiding asking whether the child wants a boy or a girl. After the baby arrives, some regression is normal; what helps most is not rushing the older child to 'act grown up,' but instead giving them daily one-on-one time and meaningful small tasks that make them feel like a helper.

1. When and how to tell an older child

The American Academy of Pediatrics (HealthyChildren, updated February 10, 2026) offers specific guidance:

  • Find a natural moment—there is no fixed week. When the news comes up naturally in conversation, that is the right time.
  • Use time markers the child can perceive. Young children do not understand "in six months." Instead say "when leaves start falling" or "when it gets cold outside."
  • Avoid asking "do you want a boy or a girl?" Whatever the answer, it may not match reality and can create expectations that are hard to manage later.
  • Read books about siblings together. Stories about becoming a big brother or sister help children rehearse the feeling before the event actually happens.
  • Time major changes carefully. If the older child needs to move from a crib to a bed, change rooms, or stop using diapers, the AAP recommends completing these transitions well before the birth or waiting until things have settled after the baby arrives.

MedlinePlus (National Library of Medicine) also notes: tell your older child before extended family discusses the news widely. Hearing it from someone else first can feel like being left out.

2. What to expect after the baby arrives

Some regression is normal. An older child who was sleeping through the night or toilet-trained may temporarily revert. The AAP's guidance is straightforward: do not say "you are big now, that is for babies." Instead, acknowledge the feeling ("I know things feel different right now") and provide:

  • Daily exclusive time. Even 10–15 minutes of one-on-one time—reading together, playing a game, talking—without the baby present helps the older child feel that the relationship has not changed.
  • Small meaningful tasks. Letting the older child help carry a fresh diaper, sing to the baby, or fold a cloth does not overwhelm them but gives them a sense of belonging in the new family arrangement.
  • Praise maturity naturally, not comparatively. "You remembered to bring the towel—that really helped!" works better than "see, you are so grown up compared to the baby."

3. How a "becoming a big sibling" picture book can help

A book where the older child is the main character learning to become a big sibling functions as a rehearsal space. The child can practice saying the character's name (their own name), try out the character's actions (holding the baby gently, speaking quietly), and decide how the character feels. Reading the book more than once before the birth gives them both a script and a sense that they are not alone.

FAQ

Q1: When is the right time to tell an older child about a new sibling?
There is no fixed week. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends choosing a natural moment and using language the child can relate to in perceivable terms—such as 'when it gets cold outside'—rather than months. Tell the child before extended family discusses the news widely.
Q2: Is regression normal after a new sibling arrives?
Yes. The AAP notes that regression in sleep, toileting, or behavior is common and normal. Avoid saying 'you are big now.' Provide daily one-on-one time, small meaningful tasks, and natural praise for mature behavior.
Q3: Can a picture book about becoming a big sibling help?
Yes. A book where the older child is the main character helps them rehearse the transition before it happens. Reading it multiple times before the birth gives children both a script and a sense they are prepared.
