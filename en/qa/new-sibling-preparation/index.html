A New Baby Is Coming: How to Prepare an Older Child, and How Picture Books Can Help

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-16   ·  中文

The short answer

To help an older child prepare to welcome a new sibling, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends three things: tell them early in a way they can understand — such as reading together picture books about newborns and becoming a big sibling; aim to complete major changes like potty training or moving from a crib to a bed before the new baby is born, and if that's not possible, wait until after the baby is settled at home; and after the baby arrives, set aside a daily period that belongs only to the older child.

1. Before the baby: let the older child prepare gradually

Find a natural moment to tell them. AAP does not specify a pregnancy week to tell the child; it recommends finding a natural opening, such as when the child starts asking "Why is Mum's tummy getting bigger?" or when you're shopping for baby clothes together. (AAP HealthyChildren) The U.S. National Library of Medicine's MedlinePlus also recommends that the child hear the news from you before everyone around them is talking about it. (MedlinePlus)

Explain time in ways the child can feel. Toddlers don't yet understand "three months from now." MedlinePlus recommends using something the child can sense, such as "When the weather gets cold, the baby will come." It also suggests not asking the child "Do you want a brother or a sister?" to avoid disappointment.

Read together about newborns and siblings. AAP recommends reading picture books about newborns and siblings together with toddlers and preschoolers, to help the child become familiar with words like "big sister," "little brother," and "new baby."

Schedule major changes away from the birth. AAP recommends completing potty training or moving from a crib to a bed before the baby is born if possible; if not, wait until after the baby is settled at home.

2. After the baby: the older child needs to feel seen

Expect some regression. AAP notes the older child may have accidents or want a bottle again. Rather than saying "you're already grown up," praise them when they act like a big kid.

Set aside daily exclusive time. Reading together, playing, or chatting all count — the key is that this time belongs only to them. When relatives visit to see the baby, also ask them to spend a little time with the older child.

Let them help. Fetching diapers or singing to the baby gives the older child a sense of being part of the team rather than left out.

Tell them about when they were little. MedlinePlus mentions that children love hearing their own story: bring out photos from the pregnancy and their infancy, and tell them about things you did together.

3. Questions to ask during shared reading with the older child

1. "When the baby cries, what do you think they want?" 2. "How could you help?" 3. "Were you like this when you were little?" (then bring out their baby photos) 4. "How do you feel right now? Being a bit nervous is fine." Let the older child know that saying "I'm a little nervous" is okay.

FAQ

Q1: When should you tell the older child they're getting a sibling?
The American Academy of Pediatrics does not specify a fixed number of weeks; it recommends finding a natural moment, such as when the child asks about Mum's tummy or when you're shopping for baby things together. MedlinePlus also recommends that the child hear the news from the parents before everyone around them is talking about it.
Q2: What if the older child regresses after the new baby arrives?
AAP notes that regression is common — for example, accidents or wanting a bottle again. The recommendation is not to say "you're already grown up" but instead to praise them when they act like a big kid, and to set aside a daily period that belongs only to the older child.
Q3: Should potty training happen before or after the new baby's birth?
AAP recommends completing potty training or the transition to a bed before the baby is born if possible; if not, wait until after the baby is settled at home.
