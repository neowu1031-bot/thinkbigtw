How to Read a World-Travel Picture Book: Guiding a Child From Story to World Map

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-11-03   ·  中文

The short answer

When reading a world map with a child, start by finding your own home, then find one location from a picture book, connecting the story with the location. Getting to know one stop at a time and leaving one question to ask is enough to begin — no need to memorize country names or capitals first. The following are shared reading activity suggestions, not age-based geography ability standards.

1. Start by finding it, then gradually talk about distance

First take out a travel picture book the child is willing to browse, then get a map with clear place names. An adult can first point to "we live here" and then find the place the story is visiting. If the child only wants to look at the vehicles, start by talking about how to get there; no need to explain borders, directions, and distances all at the same time — stopping at one page today is fine.

The Ministry of Education's Preschool Curriculum Framework lists "gathering information, organizing information, and solving problems" as three abilities in the cognitive domain, and discusses using images to record observations. (Ministry of Education curriculum framework attachment, cognitive domain) The "finding locations" and "drawing routes" activities below are family activities designed along those lines — they are not curriculum-specified teaching materials, nor do they imply that children must already understand abstract maps.

2. Make a "travel card" with a one-stop story

  1. Pick one image. Ask the child to select the page they most want to stay on. First say what they see — there's water, a tall tower, many windows — no need to name the location yet.
  2. Find the location together. The adult reads out the place name from the book and finds it on the map to point out to the child; when it can't be found, say "let's look this up" rather than guessing.
  3. Leave a route. On your own piece of paper, draw home and the destination, connecting them with a line; draw the preferred mode of transport next to it. This picture is a story record, not a to-scale travel map.
  4. Leave one question. For example, "What do people there eat for breakfast?" Write the question on the back of the card first, then find related library books later. Don't draw conclusions about an entire place's way of life from just one scenic image.

If the child wants to connect all the places in one day, you can respond "this is an imaginary journey" and then say that for a real trip, routes still need to be checked. Imaginary stories can be freely drawn; facts like the spelling of place names on the book and where photos were taken should be verified separately. This arrangement is an editorial suggestion — parents can reduce steps based on the child's interest that day.

3. Beside the map, leave a little room for the unknown

It's recommended to keep "what we see," "what we guess," and "what we want to ask" separate. For example, "there's a tower in the picture" is an observation; "it's very hot there every day" needs to be verified with another source. When talking about people from different places, you can also ask "what else do you want to know about how they live?" — avoiding treating one storybook as a complete introduction to an entire country or culture.

The AAP's shared reading recommendations encourage stopping to talk about illustrations and asking what might happen next. You can also draw one more stop for the story and let the child decide where the next page goes. When a world map isn't appealing, start with Taiwan-themed shared reading; for children who like vehicles, next try the observation activities for vehicle lovers.

To keep a card, let the child choose which envelope to put it in, and draw this trip's destination on the outside. The next time you bring it out, first ask what image they remember, then add one new question. This is just a proposal for organizing shared reading — there's no need to complete an entire world map, and not having visited a place doesn't mean you're behind.

FAQ

Q1: Does a child need to be able to read before looking at maps?
The activities here can have the adult read out place names while the child points to the image or draws; the ability to read is not a condition for participation.
Q2: Do we need to buy a very detailed world map?
Start with a map you already have or can borrow, confirmed by an adult to have the place names you need; starting with one stop is enough.
Q3: Can the itinerary in the picture book be followed for a real trip?
The story route can be used as conversation material; for an actual trip, locations, transportation, and itinerary conditions should still be confirmed separately.
