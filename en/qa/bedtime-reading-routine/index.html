How to Start Bedtime Reading: 4 Steps to Build a Lasting Bedtime Routine

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-21   ·  中文

The short answer

A bedtime reading routine is easier to start than to sustain. The key is making it short, predictable, and happening every night. Taiwan's Health Promotion Administration recommends a fixed pre-sleep sequence (toilet → bath → pajamas → teeth → water → bed → story → goodnight → lights out); the American Academy of Pediatrics calls the same idea 'brush, book, bed.' The story step does not need to be long: five to ten minutes of reading together, then a fixed closing phrase, is more valuable than an occasional thirty-minute session.

1. Why routine matters more than duration

The Taiwan Health Promotion Administration recommends a bedtime ritual sequence for young children: toilet → bath → pajamas → teeth → water → bed → story → goodnight phrase → lights out. This fixed sequence acts as a signal chain: each step tells the child’s nervous system that sleep is coming next. Studies of sleep routines in young children consistently find that predictable pre-sleep sequences are associated with faster sleep onset and fewer night wakings than variable routines—even when duration is similar.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (HealthyChildren) summarizes the concept simply: "brush, book, bed"—three things, in that order, every night from infancy onward.

2. What to do if the child wants the same book every night

Taiwan's Ministry of Education Bookstart handbook (2021 edition) notes that children aged 2–3 often prefer the same book repeated many times rather than new books. This is developmentally normal—repetition is how they consolidate language and story structure. When a child says "again," that is a learning signal, not a failure of variety.

Practically: keep 3–5 books in the bedtime rotation and let the child choose. When a book has been read so many times you know every word, that is when you can introduce a new one alongside the old favorite.

3. Four steps to build a bedtime routine from scratch

Step 1: Set a consistent bedtime. Children 1–5 typically need 10–13 hours of sleep in a 24-hour period (including naps). Pick a bedtime that leaves enough time for the full routine plus sleep, and hold to it on weekdays and weekends.

Step 2: Create a fixed quiet sequence before lights out. Follow the HPA sequence above, or adapt it to your household. The story step belongs near the end—after the physically active steps (bath, teeth) but before lights out.

Step 3: Story goes in bed, not on the couch. Starting the book in the bedroom—ideally with the child already lying down—anchors the association: bed → story → sleep. This makes the transition from "book time" to "sleep time" nearly seamless.

Step 4: Use a fixed closing phrase. When the book is done, have a phrase you say every night: "Goodnight, [child's name]. Sleep well. I'll see you in the morning." The same words, the same tone, every night. This phrase becomes the signal that the story is over and sleep begins.

FAQ

Q1: How do I build a bedtime reading routine for my child?
Four steps: set a consistent bedtime, create a fixed quiet pre-sleep sequence (bath, teeth, pajamas, water, bed, story), read the story in bed with the child already lying down, and end with the same closing phrase every night. Consistency matters more than duration.
Q2: What if my child wants the same bedtime book every night?
This is normal for children aged 2–3. Repetition consolidates language and story structure. Keep 3–5 books in rotation and let the child choose; introduce a new book alongside an old favorite once the current favorite has been read many times.
Q3: What is 'brush, book, bed'?
It is the American Academy of Pediatrics' simplified label for a consistent pre-sleep routine: brush teeth, read a book, go to bed—in that order, every night from infancy onward.
Q4: How long should bedtime reading last?
Five to ten minutes is enough if it happens every night. A consistent short routine is more effective than occasional long sessions. The goal is predictability, not duration.
