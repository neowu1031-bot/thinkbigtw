What Difference Does It Make to Put a Child's Name in a Story?

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-22   ·  中文

The short answer

Writing a name into a story is better suited as an opening that grabs a child's attention and makes them feel the story is theirs; there is no research yet proving that "having their name in the book" makes children love reading more or learn better. Research has found that infants as young as about 4.5 months old already prefer to hear the sound of their own name; 5-month-old infants pay more attention to objects they see after hearing their own name, though these are infant responses measured in a laboratory.

1. What two studies found

StudyChild's ageWhat was done and found
Mandel, Jusczyk & Pisoni (1995)
Psychological Science 6(5), 314–317		About 4.5 monthsUsed a "head-turn preference" experiment to compare infant responses to hearing their own name versus other names (including names with the same stress pattern). Infants showed a clear preference for their own name — meaning they listened to their own name longer than other names. (Journal page, University at Buffalo press release)
Parise, Friederici & Striano (2010)
PLoS ONE 5(12), e14208		5 monthsMeasured brain waves and found that infants' brain responses could distinguish their own name from a stranger's name from the very start of the name; after hearing their own name, they also paid more attention to the objects that appeared next. The authors concluded that 5-month-old infants not only recognize their own name but also use it as a social cue that guides their attention. (PubMed Central full text)

2. What these studies did not say

They did not test "name in a book." Both studies had infants listen to the sound of a name, not read a book. Neither study answers whether having a name in a book title or story makes children more engaged or learn better.

They tested infants. The subjects were infants about 5 months old; how older children feel when they see their name written in a book cannot be directly inferred from this.

For research on books where the child is the main character and the limitations of that research, see Do books where the child is the main character really help with reading?

3. How parents can use the name during shared reading

Use the name to wake up attention. When turning to a new page, say the child's name first and then point to the illustration — echoing the research finding that infants pay more attention to what they see next after hearing their own name.

Use the name to bring the child into the story. When reading about something the main character does, ask "If it were [child's name], what would you do?" and let the child continue in their own words.

Nicknames work too. Whatever the child normally hears, use that; the name in the book does not need to be the full registered name.

FAQ

Q1: At what age do infants recognize their own name?
Mandel et al.'s 1995 study found that infants around 4.5 months old listened to their own name longer than other names; Parise et al.'s 2010 study found that 5-month-old infants' brain responses could distinguish their own name from a stranger's name.
Q2: Does writing a child's name in a book make them love reading more?
There is no research that directly proves this. The two studies above measured infant responses to hearing a name, not the effect of having a name in a book. A name is better suited as an opening that grabs attention and makes the child feel the story is theirs.
Q3: What happens when an infant hears their own name?
Five-month-old infants pay more attention to objects that appear next after hearing their own name. The study authors believed they treat their own name as a social cue that guides their attention.
