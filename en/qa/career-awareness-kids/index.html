My Child Says They Want to Be Something When They Grow Up: How to Nurture Career Curiosity

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-11-01   ·  中文

The short answer

When a child says 'I want to be a firefighter,' the best response is not to test whether they know what firefighters do, but to explore the idea with them. Taiwan's National Academy for Educational Research (NAER) career planning education guidelines place the elementary school stage at 'awareness cultivation'—not career selection. At preschool age, the goal is even earlier: developing curiosity about the roles people play in the community and understanding that different kinds of work exist.

1. Taiwan curriculum framework for career awareness

NAER's October 2020 Issue Integration Handbook for Career Planning Education defines three progressive stages:

  • Elementary school: Self-concept development and career awareness cultivation. Focus on personal traits, interests, and curiosity about work and educational environments.
  • Junior high: Decision-making awareness and career exploration. Focus on connecting interests to possible pathways.
  • Senior high: Career and education interest exploration and planning.

At the elementary level, the specific competency indicators include: understanding different life roles (涯E2), identifying personal traits and interests (涯E4), showing curiosity about different work environments (涯E8), and understanding that different types of work and educational environments exist (涯E9).

Taiwan's Ministry of Education Preschool Curriculum Guidelines social domain identifies the community as one context for learning: understanding the roles of community helpers (police, doctors, delivery workers, teachers) and exploring relationships with people, things, and events in the living environment.

The key point from both sources: at preschool and early elementary age, the emphasis is on awareness and curiosity, not on narrowing toward a specific career. Saying "I want to be an astronaut" is excellent at age 4—it means the child is imagining themselves doing meaningful work.

2. How to respond when a child announces a career dream

Ask, don’t evaluate. "What do you think firefighters do every day?" is better than "do you know that firefighters have to do a lot of training?" The first invites imagination; the second introduces a gatekeeping test.

Add one real detail. Look up one thing together: what a veterinarian's day looks like, what an astronaut eats in space, what tools a chef uses. One real detail anchors the fantasy in reality without replacing it.

Connect to something they can do now. "Vets take care of animals. What could we do to take care of [pet's name] better?" This turns career curiosity into an action today, which is developmentally the right level.

Normalize change. Children change their career dreams frequently. Treat each one as a window into their current interests and values, not a prediction of the future. Research on career development consistently finds that early career interests (even in elementary school) are weakly predictive of adult career choice.

FAQ

Q1: When should children start thinking about careers?
In Taiwan's curriculum framework, elementary school focuses on awareness and curiosity—not career selection. Preschool age is about understanding that community helpers and different kinds of workers exist. Career decision-making only begins formally in junior high.
Q2: How should I respond when my child says they want to be an astronaut?
Ask open-ended questions ('what do you think astronauts do every day?'), add one real detail you look up together, and connect it to something they can do now. Treat career dreams at this age as windows into current interests and values, not binding decisions.
Q3: Is it normal for children to change their career dreams?
Yes. Research on career development finds that early career interests are weakly predictive of adult career choice. Frequent changes are developmentally normal—each one reflects the child's current interests and imagination.
