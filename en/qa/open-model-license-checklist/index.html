Can Open Models Be Used Commercially? Four License Checks Before a Company Adopts One

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-19   ·  中文

The short answer

Not always. It depends on the license of the exact version you download, not on the model's reputation or the word "open". Before adopting one, check four things: whether the license allows commercial use, whether there is a user-count or revenue threshold, whether there is an acceptable use policy (a list of prohibited uses), and what conditions apply to derivative models and redistribution. Versions within the same model family can carry different licenses.

1. "Open" does not mean "anything goes"

In everyday use, "open-source model" usually means a model whose weights you can download and run on your own hardware; "open-weight" is the more precise term. Being downloadable does not mean there are no conditions. The conditions are in the license file that comes with each model.

Licenses fall roughly into two groups:

  • General permissive open-source licenses, such as the Apache License 2.0: they grant royalty-free rights to reproduce, prepare derivative works, and distribute. Redistribution requires including a copy of the license, marking modified files as changed, and retaining existing copyright and license notices.
  • Licenses or terms written by the model provider: these may add user-scale thresholds, prohibited uses, attribution rules, or naming rules for derivative models.

So the answer to "can we use it commercially?" always comes from the official license text for that version.

2. Four checks before adoption

Check What to look for Why it matters
1. Commercial use allowed Whether the grant is restricted to research or non-commercial use Some models allow only research or personal use
2. User-count or revenue threshold Above what number of users or revenue a separate license is required Most small and mid-sized companies will not reach it, but confirm whether it exists and where it sits
3. Acceptable use policy The prohibited-use list, and whether it is a condition of the license Breaching a prohibited use can mean breaching the license
4. Derivative models and redistribution How fine-tuned models must be named, whether attribution is required, what must accompany distribution Installing a model in a system delivered to a customer may count as redistribution

Read each model's official license page yourself: find the license file (usually LICENSE) on the model's official download page or repository and match it to the version number you actually downloaded.

3. Record licenses in the component inventory

OWASP's supply chain guidance for LLM applications (LLM03:2025) recommends inventorying all license types involved with a bill of materials (BOM) and keeping an up-to-date software bill of materials (SBOM). In practice, for each system you should be able to look up:

  1. Which model, which version, and where it was downloaded from.
  2. The license name for that version and a copy of the license file.
  3. The licenses of the tools and packages that run the model, recorded separately from the model license.
  4. The result of the four checks, with who confirmed it and when.

When a model is updated, read the new version's license again. Licensing involves legal judgment; before commercial use, have legal counsel confirm against your actual use.

FAQ

Q1: Can all open-source models be used commercially for free?
Not always. Being able to download the weights does not mean there are no conditions. Check the license of the exact version: some allow only research use, and some have user-scale thresholds or prohibited-use lists.
Q2: How do you confirm whether a model can be used commercially?
Find the license file on the model's official download page or repository, match it to the version you downloaded, and check four points: commercial use, user-count or revenue thresholds, acceptable use policy, and derivative and redistribution conditions.
Q3: Can a fine-tuned model be sold or delivered to customers?
It depends on the original license's terms for derivative models and redistribution, such as naming rules, attribution requirements, and documents that must accompany distribution. Installing a model in a system delivered to a customer may count as redistribution.
Q4: How should a company manage the model licenses it uses?
Record model, version, download source, license file, and check results in the component inventory (SBOM), record tool and package licenses separately, and recheck when models are updated. Have legal counsel confirm before commercial use.
