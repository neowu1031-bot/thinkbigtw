Is It Safe to Post Children's Photos Online? 4 Things to Consider Before You Share

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-11-07   ·  中文

The short answer

Before deciding whether to post a child's photos online, it is recommended to first consider whether the child is willing, who can see them, what information the image reveals, and how to handle deletion later — then decide whether to share. UNICEF notes that online sharing cannot be entirely risk-free; parents can choose photo by photo and do not need to make everything public. The following are family privacy suggestions for reference and do not constitute legal advice.

1. First distinguish between keeping photos, sharing with family, and sharing publicly

Keeping photos at home, sharing them in a family group chat, and posting them on a public page are three different arrangements. UNICEF's sharenting page includes video sharing on messaging platforms in its discussion, not just public social media. So the next time you want to share a photo, you can first ask "who do I actually want to see this?" before deciding where to post it.

When a child is old enough to express their wishes, it is recommended to show them the selected photo and explain who it will be shared with; if they say they don't want it shared, choose a different one or don't share. For young children who cannot yet understand the consequences of sharing, UNICEF notes they cannot give informed consent, and parents still need to consider their privacy and feelings on their behalf. This should not be simplified to "the child nodded, so any use is fine."

2. Ask yourself these 4 questions before pressing send

What to considerCheck you can do at home
How will the child see this in the future?Start by not sharing images of nudity, embarrassment, uncontrolled crying, or private caregiving; when in doubt, keep it for yourself.
Who is actually the audience?Check the account visibility settings, group members, and album permissions once — not just relying on the impression that "this is a private account."
What information does it reveal?Check for name tags, school badges, addresses, schedule text, and other people's faces; unnecessary information can be cropped out or omitted.
What if you change your mind?Confirm whether you can delete the post and how to ask family members to remove it; agree with family in advance to ask before forwarding.

This checklist is organized based on UNICEF's reminders about audience, identifiable information, embarrassing images, and communication with family and friends; it is not a test where passing all four items eliminates risk. You can also first share a photo of a craft the child made without showing their face, saying only what you did together that day — putting the emphasis on the experience rather than the image.

3. Even if you've already shared, you can adjust starting today

You can start by reviewing recent albums, selecting content that no longer needs to be public, and deleting or adjusting visibility photo by photo; then tell family members "please ask us before forwarding photos of the children in the future." This is a sample family agreement — it doesn't require sorting through all old photos at once, nor does it place all responsibility on one caregiver.

If you are submitting photos to a provider to make a book, see the separate collection notification. The Personal Data Protection Act Article 8 covers the period, territory, recipients, and methods of data use; Article 3 covers rights to inquire, correct, stop use, and request deletion. (Laws and Regulations Database) Submitting to a production service and sharing publicly are different uses and cannot be equated.

For specific questions, see privacy questions before making a custom book; if what you're sharing includes photos of both adults and children, you can also refer to consent and handling of adults' photos.

When family members disagree, it is also fine to hold off on posting and discuss the specific photo together first.

FAQ

Q1: Is a private group chat safe enough for sharing?
It is still recommended to confirm the members and forwarding agreement. Even private sharing requires considering the child's feelings and identifiable information.
Q2: If the child says they don't want a photo shared publicly, what should I do?
Their wishes can be respected — keep it for yourself or choose different content; there is no need to make sharing a condition of their cooperation in taking photos.
Q3: If I've shared a lot of photos before, can I sort through them now?
You can start by reviewing recent content, deleting posts you no longer need to keep public, then discuss future sharing practices with family members.
Q4: Will Tale With You post order photos on the website?
Under the Privacy Policy, order photos are not used on the website as samples; without separate written consent, customers' books are not displayed either.
