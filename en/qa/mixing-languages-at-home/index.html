Will Mixing Mandarin, Taiwanese and English at Home Confuse My Child?

Think BIG Q&A Library　·　November 17, 2026　·　中文版

Short answer

No consistent research says that mixing languages at home always confuses a child. Kaushanskaya and Crespo (2019) studied 174 Spanish-English bilingual children with an average age of 7.8 and found that the effect of hearing code-switching depends on the child's verbal working memory: for children with high working memory, more exposure was linked to higher language ability, and for children with lower working memory, to lower ability. So there is no single answer about whether mixing is good or bad. Families who want to reduce confusion often assign languages to people or situations; that is practical advice, not a finding of this study.

1.What the study did

Kaushanskaya and Crespo (2019) recruited 174 Spanish-English bilingual children with a mean age of 7.78, measured their language ability in both languages, collected parent reports of how often the children heard code-switching, and analysed this against each child's verbal working memory. 2.How to read it

An association, not a cause. The study used parent-reported frequency and did not ask families to change how they speak.

School-aged children. The average age was 7.8, so it may not apply to toddlers.

Different language pair. It was Spanish and English, not Mandarin or Taiwanese.

The point is that it depends on the child. The authors concluded that a child's cognitive processing capacity determines whether code-switching exposure helps or hinders language skills.

3.Common household approaches

These are approaches families often use. We found no research showing that any one is best, so adjust to your child:

1. One person, one language. For example, Mum speaks Mandarin and Grandma speaks Taiwanese.

2. One setting, one language. For example, Mandarin at meals and another language at bedtime.

3. Do not scold mixing. Naturally say it back in the language the moment calls for.

4. Make sure each language has enough input. Performance in each language is related to the proportion heard (see two languages and speech delay).

Frequently asked questions

Q1: Will mixing Mandarin, Taiwanese and English confuse my child?
No consistent research says it always does. Kaushanskaya and Crespo (2019) studied 174 bilingual children averaging 7.8 years and found the effect depends on the child's verbal working memory.
Q2: Is language mixing good or bad for children?
There is no single answer. In that study children with higher working memory did better with more exposure, and children with lower working memory did worse. It is an association, not a cause.
Q3: How can I reduce the chance of confusion?
Families often assign languages to people or situations and make sure each language has enough input. That is practical advice, not a finding of the study.
Q4: Should I correct my child for mixing languages?
No need to scold. Naturally say it back in the language the moment calls for.
