Shared Reading & Custom Picture Books Q20

How Much Screen Time Is Right for Under-5s? What Role Can Printed Books Play at Home

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-30   ·  中文

The short answer

The World Health Organization's (WHO) 2019 guidelines recommend: no screen time for children under 1; no prolonged sedentary screen time for 1-year-olds; no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time per day for children aged 2–4, and less is better. When children are seated, caregivers are encouraged to read and tell stories with them. The Health Promotion Administration's (NHA) 2014 guidelines on 3C product vision care state: no screen time for children under 2; no more than 1 hour per day for children aged 2 and over; take a 10-minute break after every 30 minutes of use. Physical books can be placed in time slots that were previously used for screen time, as a quiet activity done with an adult present.

WHO's recommendation comes from Guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5 years of age, published on 24 April 2019; the press release also notes that non-screen interactive activities done with a caregiver — such as reading, storytelling, singing, and puzzles — are very important for children's development. (WHO press release, WHO guidelines page)

The NHA's recommendation came from the notice on precautions for 3C product vision care established at a government-academic industry meeting on 5 June 2014. (Ministry of Health and Welfare website, source: Health Promotion Administration)

2. Where physical books fit in

Both sets of guidelines focus on "less screen time"; WHO additionally names reading and storytelling as activities to do together with a caregiver. The following are practical suggestions, not content from the above sources:

1. First identify the most common screen-time slot. For example, after dinner or when adults are doing chores. Start by changing just one slot — no need to change everything at once.

2. Replace it with an adult reading alongside. WHO specifies reading and storytelling done "with a caregiver" — giving a child a book to look at alone is different from an adult reading with them.

3. Keep books in a place the child can reach. Put away the tablet and place books at a height the child can grab.

4. Switch to reading before bed. For how to structure the bedtime routine, see How to start bedtime shared reading.

3. What if it's hard to do

WHO's phrasing is "less is better," not "failure if you go over." Starting with a little less than yesterday is enough; an adult staying present during screen time, and chatting about the content afterward, are both better than leaving the child to watch alone. FAQ

Q1: Can children under 2 watch screens?
WHO's 2019 guidelines do not recommend screen time for children under 1, and do not recommend prolonged sedentary screen time for 1-year-olds; the NHA's 2014 3C product vision care notice states no screen time for children under 2.
Q2: How much screen time can a 3–4-year-old have per day?
WHO recommends no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time per day, and less is better; the NHA's 2014 3C product vision care notice states no more than 1 hour per day for children aged 2 and over, with a 10-minute break after every 30 minutes of use.
Q3: What does WHO recommend children do when seated?
WHO's guidelines state that when children are seated, caregivers are encouraged to read and tell stories with them; the press release also mentions singing and puzzles as non-screen interactive activities to do together with a caregiver.
