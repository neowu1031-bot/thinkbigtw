Shared Reading & Custom Picture Books Q03

Do Books Starring Your Child Really Help With Reading? What the Research Says

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-13   ·  中文

The short answer

There is early supporting research. Studies by Kucirkova and colleagues at the Open University found that 3-year-old children acquired new words faster in sections of a book where they were the main character, and that parent-child smiling, laughter, and talking were all higher when reading a book featuring the child. However, these studies are small-scale and better demonstrate that children are more engaged rather than proving a guaranteed long-term improvement in reading ability.

1. How the research was conducted

"Personalized picture books" are books made for a specific child, typically by putting the child's name, photo, or personal details into the story. The researcher who has studied this topic most extensively is Natalia Kucirkova at The Open University in the UK. Two commonly cited studies:

StudyParticipants and designFindings
Kucirkova, Messer & Sheehy (2014), First Language 3418 children aged 3. The same book contained both "personalized" and "non-personalized" sections, each containing a few unfamiliar target words. The book was read together twice over one week, then children were tested on the words.Children learned the target words in personalized sections faster; repeated shared reading amplified the effect.
Kucirkova, Messer & Whitelock (2013), Journal of Early Childhood Literacy 13(4)7 UK families with children aged 12–33 months. Each family read three types of books at home—a personalized book made for the child, a comparable general book, and the child's favorite book—with sessions video-recorded.When reading the personalized book, children and parents showed significantly more smiling and laughter; children also vocalized more than with either other book type. Parents' smiles mostly followed the child's lead.

2. What these studies do not establish

Very small samples. One study had 18 children, the other 7 families, and both were conducted in the UK in English. The results are worth noting but cannot be generalized to all children.

The effect measured is engagement, not guaranteed ability. What was measured was the speed of short-term word acquisition and the frequency of smiles and talk. No research has demonstrated that reading personalized picture books will improve a child's long-term reading scores.

What matters most is having someone read with the child. The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2014 policy statement notes that regularly reading aloud to young children strengthens the parent-child relationship and builds the foundation for language, literacy, and social-emotional development; it recommends starting from infancy and continuing at least through kindergarten (AAP, Pediatrics 2014). A book that stars the child can be a good starting point for sitting down and reading together—not a replacement for ordinary picture books.

3. Four ways to read a "my child is the star" book effectively

1. Let the child find themselves first. Ask "where are you in this picture?" and then follow with "what are you doing here?" The child will start telling the story in their own words.

2. Ask "what would you do if you were there?" Since the main character is them, it is easier for them to voice their own thoughts—exactly the situation in which children talked more in the research.

3. Read the same book more than once. Children in the First Language study read the book twice; repeated shared reading led to better word retention. When a child says "again!", that is the opportunity.

4. Bring new words from the book into real life. If "cable car" or "ferry" appears in the book, point it out the next time you are out. That is when a word truly becomes theirs.

FAQ

Q1: What are the benefits of a picture book starring your child?
Research found that 3-year-olds learned new words faster in sections where they were the main character, and that parent-child smiling, laughter, and talking were higher when reading a personalized book. These studies are small and better demonstrate higher engagement than a guaranteed improvement in long-term reading ability.
Q2: What are the limitations of the personalized-book research?
The main studies had samples of only 18 children and 7 families, all conducted in the UK in English, measuring short-term word acquisition and interaction frequency. No research has shown it leads to better long-term reading scores.
Q3: At what age should shared reading begin?
The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2014 policy statement recommends starting from infancy and continuing at least through kindergarten, as regular shared reading strengthens the parent-child relationship and builds language, literacy, and social-emotional skills.
Q4: How do you read a child-as-protagonist book effectively?
Let the child find themselves first; ask 'what would you do if you were there'; read the same book more than once; and bring new vocabulary from the book into daily life. Research showed that repeated shared reading improved word retention.
