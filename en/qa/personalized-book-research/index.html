Shared Reading & Custom Picture Books Q03

Do Books Starring Your Child Really Help With Reading? What the Research Says

Think BIG Q&A Library  ·  2026-10-13   ·  中文

The short answer

There is preliminary research support. Studies by Kucirkova and colleagues at the Open University in the UK found that 3-year-old children learned new words faster in passages where they themselves were the main character; and when reading a book featuring the child as the main character, smiling, laughter, and talking between parent and child were also noticeably more frequent. However, these studies were very small in scale; they can better be said to show "greater engagement" rather than proving that long-term reading ability will necessarily improve as a result.

1. How the research was conducted

A "personalized picture book" refers to a book made specifically for one child — this may involve putting the child's name, photo, or personal details into the story. The researcher who has studied this topic most extensively is Natalia Kucirkova and colleagues at The Open University in the UK. Two commonly cited studies are as follows:

StudySubjects and methodsFindings
Kucirkova, Messer & Sheehy (2014)
First Language, vol. 34		18 children aged 3. The same book contained both "personalized" and "non-personalized" passages, each containing several new words the children didn't know; the book was read together twice over a week, then the children were tested on whether they understood these words.Children learned new words in the personalized passages faster; repeated shared reading made this effect more pronounced.
Kucirkova, Messer & Whitelock (2013)
Journal of Early Childhood Literacy, vol. 13 no. 4		7 families in the UK, with children aged 12 to 33 months. At home, each of three book types was read in turn: a personalized book made for the child, a similarly themed general book, and the child's favorite book; all sessions were video-recorded and analyzed.When reading the personalized book, children's and parents' smiling and laughter were noticeably more frequent; children also vocalized more than with the other two types of books. Parents' laughter was mostly in response to their child's.

2. How to interpret these results

Sample sizes are very small. One study had 18 children and one had 7 families, and both were conducted in the UK in English. The results are worth noting but cannot be directly generalized to all children.

The effect is "engagement," not a guaranteed ability outcome. What the studies measured was short-term speed of learning new words, and the frequency of smiling and talking. There is currently no research proving that "reading personalized picture books" leads to better long-term reading outcomes.

What truly matters is having someone to read with. The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2014 policy statement notes that regularly reading together with young children strengthens the parent-child relationship and builds language, literacy, and social-emotional skills; it recommends starting from infancy and continuing at least until the child enters kindergarten. (AAP, Pediatrics 2014) A book where the child is the main character can serve as a "starting point" to get the child willing to sit down and read together, rather than a replacement for general picture books.

3. 4 ways to make a book where the child is the main character work

1. Let the child find "where am I." Find themselves first, then ask: "What are you doing now?" The child will start telling the story in their own words.

2. Ask often: "If it were you, what would you do?" The main character is them, so it is easier for them to voice their own thoughts — this is exactly the context in which children talked more in the research.

3. Read the same book more than once. The children in the First Language study read it twice, and repeated shared reading improved new-word learning. When a child asks for "one more time," that's the opportunity.

4. Bring new words from the book into daily life. When a cable car or ferry appears in the book, point it out the next time you're out; that's how the word truly becomes theirs.

FAQ

Q1: What are the benefits of a picture book where the child is the main character?
Research found that 3-year-old children learned new words faster in passages where they were the main character; and when reading a book featuring the child as the main character, smiling, laughter, and talking between parent and child were noticeably more frequent. These studies were small in scale; they better show that children are more engaged rather than proving long-term reading ability will improve.
Q2: What are the limitations of personalized picture book research?
The main studies had samples of only 18 children and 7 families, both in the UK in English; what was measured was short-term word-learning speed and interaction frequency. There is currently no research proving it leads to better long-term reading outcomes.
Q3: At what age is it better to start reading with children?
The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2014 policy statement recommends starting to read with children from infancy and continuing at least until they enter kindergarten, because regular shared reading strengthens the parent-child relationship and builds language, literacy, and social-emotional skills.
Q4: How should a book where the child is the main character be read to be most effective?
Let the child first find where they are in the book; ask often "if it were you, what would you do"; read the same book multiple times; and bring new words from the book into daily life. Research showed that repeated shared reading improved new-word learning.
