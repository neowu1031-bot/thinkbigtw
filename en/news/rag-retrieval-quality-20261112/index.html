AI Keeps Missing the Point in Company Documents? Inspect Retrieval Before Changing Models

Think BIG Media  |  November 12, 2026

Review the question, retrieved passages, and answer together. Locate the failure before changing documents, retrieval settings, or the model.

One. What’s Happening: Having Documents Does Not Mean Retrieving the Right Evidence

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, retrieves relevant content before asking a model to answer from it. Microsoft’s official overview identifies terminology mismatches, content preparation, and input limits as implementation challenges.[1] Uploading files and answering questions reliably are separate steps.

Chunking is one of those steps. Official documentation describes fixed-size, structural, and semantic approaches, with size and overlap adjusted to the content and use.[2] We would not apply one character count to every Chinese policy, table, or scanned document. Read the resulting chunks to check whether they retain subjects, conditions, and exceptions.

Microsoft’s evaluation guidance separates retrieval-process quality from final-answer quality. One asks whether retrieved material is relevant; the other examines grounding, relevance, and completeness of the answer.[3] We use that distinction as a diagnostic method, without recommending a service or treating an automated score as a business decision.

Two. Perspectives: Inspect Retrieval Without Ignoring Model Behavior

The case for checking data first: The overview ties RAG quality to content preparation and retrieval relevance.[1] If the necessary evidence never reached the model, we would investigate indexing and retrieval first. A larger model does not resolve that missing input.

The model-side caution: Receiving a passage does not establish that a model uses it correctly. The original 2023 study “Lost in the Middle” found position-dependent performance in its multi-document question-answering and key-value retrieval tasks.[4] It does not establish identical behavior in every newer model. It does challenge adding more context as a universal remedy.

Test both stages: whether the evidence was retrieved and whether the answer used it faithfully. When documents conflict, ask their owner to determine which is valid rather than asking the model to settle company policy.

Add explicit failure definitions: missing exceptions, treating an old version as current, or citing the wrong document. This gives document owners and engineers shared criteria beyond fluency or a retrieval score.

Keep the original phrasing too. Rewriting conversational questions into document titles before every test can hide the terminology mismatch being tested. Save original and rewritten queries with their retrieval results before deciding whether to adopt query rewriting.

◆ First-Hand Lessons

Three. What We Learned in Practice

These observations come from our RAG document and answer-quality work. They are not industry statistics or an account of any particular organization.

1. Inspect retrieved passages first. When an answer is poor, we check the retrieved material before analyzing generation. Chunks that are too large or small are among the causes we encounter; model capability is not always the problem.

2. A readable original can produce damaged text. We have seen tables and scanned documents become corrupted during text extraction. We inspect the extracted text instead of repeatedly asking the model to read broken material better.

3. One subject may have two versions. Retrieving old and new documents together mixes evidence. We remove outdated versions or mark their status so validity can be checked.

4. Keep real questions and reference answers. We build test sets and watch for differences between conversational questions and formal document terms. Questions should reflect actual phrasing, beyond document titles.

Four. Our Recommendations: Diagnose with a Fixed Question Set

1. Inspect extracted text. Compare tables, scans, and exception clauses against originals. Check whether labels still match values. Correct preprocessing problems before rebuilding the index.

2. Map questions to evidence. Record expected answers and supporting documents and passages. Include unanswerable questions, outdated material, and terminology differences. Retrieval evaluation needs explicit relevance evidence.[3]

3. Change one class of settings at a time. Keep the model and questions fixed when comparing segmentation, overlap, or query methods. Save retrieved passages so improvements have an identifiable cause. Validate parameters on your own content.[2][3]

4. Assess evidence and answers separately. Wrong evidence points toward retrieval; correct evidence with a wrong answer calls for prompt and model checks; conflicting documents go to their owner. This is our recommended triage, beyond one aggregate score.

5. Test missing answers too. Require an explicit evidence gap when support is insufficient. Independently test access permissions so retrieved content stays within authorization.[1]

Check that the model received the right material before judging its answer.

Answer quality and document access each need acceptance checks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: What is RAG?
It retrieves relevant content and provides it to the model as evidence for an answer.[1]
Q2: Are larger chunks always better?
No. Test size, overlap, and boundaries against your content and use case.[2]
Q3: Does a citation prove the answer is correct?
Check whether it supports the claim and whether the answer is relevant and complete. These are separate evaluation dimensions.[3]
Q4: When should we consider another model?
First establish that retrieval delivers the right evidence. Then compare model and prompt choices on the same questions if answers still fail.
