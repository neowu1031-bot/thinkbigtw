Voice Impersonation · Payment Verification · Business Procedures

A Familiar Voice Asks You to Transfer Funds. Verify the Request Through a Trusted Channel

Think BIG Media  |  November 16, 2026

Pause an urgent payment request and verify both identity and purpose through previously confirmed contact details. Follow the approval workflow without first proving the audio is synthetic.

One. What’s Happening: A Familiar Voice Still Requires Verification

On May 15, 2025, the FBI warned of a campaign active since April in which actors impersonated senior US officials through texts and AI-generated voice messages to seek personal-account access.[1] This was a specific disclosed campaign, not a measure of victimization among Taiwanese businesses or evidence of a surge in executive payment fraud.

The business lesson is to separate a recognizable voice from verified identity. In an earlier warning, the FTC explained that scammers could use public audio to clone a familiar voice and advised calling a number already known to belong to the person.[3] We do not claim a universal number of seconds needed to clone a voice.

Payment requests already need verification. The FBI’s business email compromise guidance recommends confirming payment and purchase requests in person or by phone, including account-number or payment-procedure changes.[2] We extend that principle to voice requests: retain existing approvals without first needing to identify a particular synthesis technology.

Two. Perspectives: Clues Help; Procedures Are More Usable Than a Listening Test

The awareness perspective: The FBI suggests observing tone, wording, and audiovisual details while acknowledging that AI-generated content can be hard to identify.[1] Training can help people notice anomalies. In our view, detecting an audio flaw should not become the employee’s test for authorizing payment.

The procedural caution: Publicly available personal facts may be insufficient for identity verification. We recommend independently confirmed contact details and separate checks on the purpose, recipient, documents, and approver. This is a business-control proposal, not an audio-forensics method.

Another FBI announcement describes voice and real-time video impersonation uses.[4] Video may supplement checks, but an executive-looking image should not bypass payment controls. Nor should every unfamiliar recording be labeled fraudulent; apply the same verification procedure when uncertain.

Include executives in the exercise and have them explicitly accept callbacks and review delays. Staff cannot apply verification consistently if they are urged to bypass it. Record missing contacts, outdated directories, and incomplete documents as actions, with owners confirming corrections.

◆ Deployment Checklist

Three. Our Deployment Checklist

These are proposed preparation questions for businesses, not claims of victimization or successful interception.

1. Where does the contact list come from? Ask whether payment contacts and executive numbers were previously verified and who can edit them. Calling a number supplied in the suspicious message keeps verification inside the sender’s channel.[1][2]

2. Who may pause the request? We recommend explicitly allowing staff to wait for verification and naming a backup reviewer. “In a meeting and unable to call” should not create an exception.

3. What can the reviewer inspect? Give reviewers the payment purpose, original documents, and account-change history. Separate confirming identity from approving the request itself; one callback should not silently stand for both.

4. Where is help after submission? Prepare bank and internal incident contacts in advance. Limit access to preserved recordings and screenshots rather than spreading personal information through group chats.

Four. Our Recommendations: Write Steps People Can Follow

1. Pause and use a trusted channel. End the suspicious call and contact the person through previously confirmed details, rather than a new number or link in the message. The FBI and FTC advise independent verification.[1][3]

2. Verify account changes separately. Check changed payment details or procedures through established contacts and record the outcome.[2] We recommend the same procedure even when the voice sounds familiar or the message appears in an existing conversation.

3. Separate processing and review. Assign a reviewer and record the verification channel and approval basis for urgent requests too. If no reviewer is available, wait rather than treating urgency as an exception.

4. Run a tabletop exercise. Use a written scenario involving secrecy, urgency, and a new account. Check whether staff can find contacts, pause the request, and escalate. No voice clone is needed to test the process.

5. Contact the bank immediately after a mistaken transfer. The FBI recommends immediate contact with the financial institution and coordination with the receiving institution.[2] Preserve messages, times, and transaction records and seek appropriate assistance under the incident process. Do not wait for proof of AI use or assume funds will be recovered.

A voice delivers a request; verification and approval authorize a payment.

◆ Editor’s Note · How Think BIG Approaches This: Put Pause Authority in the Procedure

Let staff verify requests and find the right escalation contact.

(The following is Think BIG’s practical position, separate from the reporting above.)

We recommend using AI to organize questions or verification tasks while designated people retain payment approval. External-action workflows should preserve verification evidence, review results, and response contacts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Is a familiar voice enough?
No. The FBI and FTC recommend independent verification through known contact details.[1][3]
Q2: Must we prove AI use before pausing?
We recommend pausing for verification. The FBI’s payment-verification advice does not depend on proving AI use first.[2]
Q3: Does video make approval safe?
Do not rely on it alone. The FBI also describes real-time video impersonation.[4]
Q4: What if funds have already been sent?
Contact the bank immediately through a known channel, preserve records, and seek assistance under the incident process. The FBI recommends immediate financial-institution contact.[2]
