Was This Image Made with AI? — What Content Credentials Can Tell You

Think BIG Media  |  November 18, 2026

Read what a credential records and who signed it, then verify the claim made by the image. Missing credentials do not establish AI use. Teams need an intake and publication checklist.

One.The Shift Toward Provenance

Can appearance alone tell us whether AI was used? Provenance offers another route. C2PA says its conformance program and official trust list launched in mid-2025 to check implementation and security requirements. This article follows that development; it is not a report of a new announcement today.[1]

Content Credentials can record creation, editing, and tools used, with digitally signed provenance data. The C2PA FAQ describes a checkable history rather than a visual clue that readers must interpret from appearance.[2]

Two limits matter. The C2PA 2.2 explainer separates provenance from factual truth. CAI documentation also says missing credentials are not evidence that an image is inauthentic. A valid record and an accurate story answer different questions.[3][4]

Two.The Case for Transparency, and Its Limits

The benefit: inspectable evidence. The conformance program evaluates credential implementations and publishes a list of conforming products.[1] In our reading, this gives teams concrete questions to ask about supported workflows instead of accepting a broad claim that AI content is labeled.

The limitation: history may be incomplete. CAI notes that a normal screenshot does not carry the original image’s C2PA metadata.[4] C2PA describes soft bindings such as watermarking or fingerprinting to help recover credentials, but that does not mean every forwarded image has a complete history.[2]

We support using credentials as one part of verification while retaining editorial checks. Signing information, the depicted claim, the caption, and permission to use the asset need separate review. One successful technical check should not be extended to the entire story.[3]

We suggest three review fields: provenance present in the file, checks actually performed by the validator, and facts independently checked by the editor. Do not let one field stand in for another. A recorded creation time still needs to be compared with the event date in the caption. This design makes the evidence boundary visible instead of adding an unexplained approval badge.[3]

◆ First-Hand Lessons

Three. What We Learned in Practice

These are questions we would ask during adoption, not a completed C2PA deployment or an account involving a particular client.

1. Do we have the original file? Ask where the asset came from, whether the team has only a screenshot, and whether the original is available. Keep an identifiable file location so reviewers and publishers inspect the same version.

2. Who interprets the result? Assign responsibility for checking the signer, verifying the caption, and handling an unverifiable file. Record missing data, unsupported data, and failed validation separately rather than labeling all three as a fabricated image.

3. What will readers see? Agree on a visible source and AI-use statement if the publication surface does not show credentials. Assign a new check when the file changes. This is a workflow proposal, not a claim that every platform preserves metadata.

Four.Our Recommendations: Four Checks

1. Record the source at intake. Keep the provider, acquisition date, original file location, and intended use. Ask whether the delivery is an original or an exported copy. Track permission separately.

2. Match the record to the file. Use a credential-aware tool and save the date and result. Distinguish signature checks, trust status, and recorded edits. Ask the provider about unclear fields instead of filling gaps with assumptions.[1][2]

3. Verify factual claims separately. List people, locations, dates, and event claims. Keep checks with the original publisher and caption review even when provenance validates. This is our editorial recommendation based on the specification’s limits.[3]

4. Test the publishing path. Run a sample through the actual crop, export, and upload process, then download and inspect it. If provenance does not survive, add an understandable explanation and retain the original and review record.[4]

For handover, run one test asset from original-file intake through inspection, editing, export, upload, and download, then have another editor inspect the final file. Record filenames and results. If only a screenshot is available, say the original was unavailable rather than reporting that no AI use was found. This proposed drill checks how the team handles unknowns. Public wording should match available evidence even under publication pressure.

◆ Editor’s Note · How Think BIG Approaches This: Keep Evidence Before Publication

Check disclosure, signatures, and facts separately.

(The following is Think BIG’s practical position, separate from the reporting above.)

Think BIG’s position is to retain the source, AI-use disclosure, and publication review together. The checklist should distinguish confirmed facts from unknowns and identify who can approve use.

We do not treat a badge as a factual verdict. When evidence is incomplete, narrow the wording to what it supports. The next editor should be able to inspect the reasoning behind the decision.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Does C2PA tell us whether an image is true?
No. The explainer separates provenance from factual accuracy. Verify the depicted event separately.[3]
Q2: Does a missing credential prove AI use?
No. CAI says absence of credentials is not a negative authenticity signal.[4]
Q3: Does a screenshot preserve the original credential?
A normal screenshot does not contain the original C2PA metadata. Recovery mechanisms have separate requirements.[4][2]
Q4: Does a credential grant usage rights?
No. Provenance differs from DRM and access control. Confirm permission separately.[2]
