Taiwan’s Artificial Intelligence Basic Act — Start with Uses, Data, and Owners

Think BIG Media  |  November 28, 2026

Inventory AI uses, data, and approval owners. The Act sets a governance direction; specific obligations still require checking applicable laws and sector guidance. This is not legal advice.

One.Read the Enacted Law in Layers

Taiwan promulgated the Artificial Intelligence Basic Act on January 14, 2026. Article 20 provides for implementation from promulgation.[1] The Executive Yuan’s May 21, 2026 implementation plan describes interagency work and risk management.[2] This article interprets that existing development using sources checked on October 26, 2026.

Start with the subject of each provision. Article 4 addresses principles for government promotion of AI development and use, including human autonomy, privacy, security, transparency, fairness, and accountability. Article 16 addresses a risk-classification framework and sector authorities’ rules.[1] NSTC also publishes an English text for bilingual reference.[4]

Other laws still matter. Article 1 preserves the application of other laws to matters the Act does not cover.[1] For example, Article 5 of the Personal Data Protection Act limits personal-data activities to what is necessary for a specified purpose.[3] Studying emerging AI rules is no reason to defer existing data obligations.

Two.A Clearer Direction Still Requires Specific Checks

The benefit: development and risk share a framework. The Executive Yuan’s plan discusses industry development alongside social welfare, rights, and interagency management.[2] Our reading is that businesses can use this vocabulary to discuss purpose, data, access, and responsibility together.

The caution: summaries cannot decide individual cases. Under Article 5, high-risk advisory notices or warnings involve identification by the relevant central sector authority in consultation with the Ministry of Digital Affairs.[1][4] That should not be rewritten as an identical labeling specification for every AI output. Check the application and applicable sector rules.

Also distinguish government regulatory adjustment from a business grace period. The implementation plan discusses government reviews of laws and administrative measures.[2] We do not interpret that as permission to defer existing obligations. Specific cases need qualified legal review of the facts and effective provisions. This article is for reference and is not legal advice.

◆ First-Hand Lessons

Three. What We Learned in Practice

These are questions we would ask during adoption, not a completed legal-compliance engagement or an account of any particular client.

1. What decisions does AI make? Describe actions such as organizing files, proposing drafts, or executing external work. Ask whose interests are affected, who reviews the result, and how errors are corrected. Record facts before assigning a legal risk category.

2. Where does data come from and go? Document sources, purpose, recipients, and retention. “Internal use” is insufficient detail: identify who can read or edit the information and who handles it when it is no longer needed.

3. Who tracks rules and handles concerns? Assign owners for official updates, correction requests, and pausing work. Connect technical configuration with legal assessment without presenting the checklist as a self-issued compliance certificate.

Four.Our Recommendations: Prepare Usable Records

1. Inventory uses. Record inputs, outputs, affected parties, departments, and owners, including outputs sent externally. This is our preparation method, not a claim that the Act prescribes this exact form.

2. Examine data necessity. Ask whether names, contact details, or complete source files are needed and which fields can be removed. Review the case against applicable provisions, including Article 5 of the Personal Data Protection Act.[3]

3. Define review and correction. For outputs affecting others, assign review, a way to raise concerns, and stopping conditions. Make instructions usable by operators rather than leaving them in a governance presentation.

4. Check effective requirements. Identify the sector authority, notice date, and scope. Read commencement notes in the Laws and Regulations Database as well. The Personal Data Protection Act page checked for this article marks some amendments as not yet effective; do not treat every displayed amendment as an operative rule.[3]

5. Keep reassessment records. Recheck when purposes, data, or service terms change. Track legal questions, owners, and status separately. Check official information again before publication or operational adoption.

The inventory should distinguish confirmed legal requirements from voluntary internal controls. For the former, record the provision, applicability reasoning, and reviewer; for the latter, describe the risk and procedure. Do not present internal recommendations as statutory duties. This distinction helps identify what needs reassessment when rules change.

◆ Editor’s Note · How Think BIG Approaches This: Make Governance Maintainable

Document purpose and ownership, then check applicable requirements.

(The following is Think BIG’s practical position, separate from the reporting above.)

Think BIG’s position is that governance records should reflect operations: a clear purpose, traceable data flow, identifiable approvers, and owners for problems. Update them when the system changes.

We organize technical facts for the appropriate people to assess legal applicability. A completed installation is not a compliance determination, and an internal checklist is not regulatory approval.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Has Taiwan promulgated the Act?
Yes, on January 14, 2026. Article 20 provides for implementation from promulgation.[1]
Q2: Does every company need the same AI label?
The Act does not support that blanket conclusion. Article 5’s high-risk warnings involve official identification. Check the application and relevant rules.[1][4]
Q3: Does the Act remove personal-data obligations?
No. Article 1 preserves other applicable laws. Review data necessity under provisions such as Personal Data Protection Act Article 5.[1][3]
Q4: What can a business prepare now?
Inventory uses, data flow, approval owners, and issue handling, then obtain appropriate review of effective rules. This is for reference, not legal advice.
