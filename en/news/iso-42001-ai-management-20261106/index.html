ISO/IEC 42001:2023 · AI Governance · Management Systems

What Is ISO/IEC 42001? Documenting How a Business Governs AI

Think BIG Media  |  November 6, 2026

Inventory AI uses, owners, and checks before discussing certification. A management system needs evidence from everyday work.

One. What’s Happening: AI Governance Is Becoming Subject to Independent Assessment

On September 5, 2025, the UK accreditation body UKAS said it was advancing accreditation work for ISO/IEC 42001 AI management system certification.[1] We use that institutional development to introduce business preparation. We do not present its historical progress as today’s status or rank certification bodies.

ISO describes ISO/IEC 42001:2023 as requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an AI management system. It applies to organizations providing or using AI products and services.[2] A business does not need to train its own model to have AI uses worth inventorying.

ISO’s public explainer covers responsibility, risk, data and system management, oversight, and improvement.[3] Our reading is that an organization should be able to explain why a use is allowed and what happens when it goes wrong. This article introduces public guidance; it is not a clause-by-clause treatment of the full standard or a certification checklist.

Two. Perspectives: A Common Framework Helps; Certificates Need Context

The case for structure: UKAS describes accreditation work as supporting consistency and credibility in AI certification.[1] ISO describes a management system in terms of policies, objectives, and processes.[2] In our view, this makes governance accountable rather than leaving each department to interpret it independently.

The limitation: ISO explains that its framework supports compliance obligations while laws and regulations still apply.[3] A certificate therefore should not be read as evidence that every answer is accurate or every use has received legal review. Management processes and task-specific checks address different questions.

ISO develops standards; external certification bodies conduct certification. Accreditation formally recognizes a certification body’s competence.[4] Keep those roles distinct. Ask about the standard edition, certified organization, covered activities, and verification contact rather than simply asking whether a supplier “has ISO.”

Begin with a bounded use, such as internal document Q&A. Ask which documents are allowed, who maintains versions, what happens without evidence, and who can suspend use. This starts the governance discussion; it does not establish conformity with the whole standard.

For external descriptions, distinguish completed work from plans. Completed checks need records; open items need owners and actions. Planning around a standard should not be worded as completed independent certification. Keep the wording consistent with actual progress.

◆ Deployment Checklist

Three. Our Deployment Checklist

This section proposes questions for deployment preparation. It does not describe a certification engagement or claim certification experience.

1. Which uses belong in scope? We would ask whether AI drafts text, retrieves documents, or participates in decisions. Write down inputs, output purposes, and recipients. If the answer is “everyone uses it,” start with one describable task.

2. Who may change the rules? Identify who approves data uploads, model or plugin changes, and who can suspend use. The point is a clear decision owner, not assigning every responsibility to one engineer.

3. What demonstrates routine execution? Keep tests, versions, incident handling, and improvement tracking under the relevant use. Records should explain decisions made at the time rather than being reconstructed only before an audit.

4. What needs specialist review? Turn contract, privacy, and sector-specific questions into explicit requests for qualified advisers. This introduction is informational, not legal advice or a conformity assessment.

Four. Our Recommendations: Start with One Use and Traceable Records

1. Inventory AI uses. Record purpose, owner, sources, users, supplier, and shutdown method. Include services actually used, beyond the purchasing list.

2. Connect risks to responses. For outdated documents, unsupported answers, or unauthorized retrieval, name the reviewer, response, and retest condition. This is our practical application of the risk-management direction in ISO’s public guidance.[3]

3. Keep change and correction records. When changing a model, document source, or tool, record the change, approval, and test questions. Track incident outcomes and open actions rather than closing a ticket with “improved.”

4. Confirm certification needs separately. Discuss scope, procedures, and evidence with the certification body when required. ISO describes certification as voluntary, while some industries may have contractual or legal requirements; assess the specific situation.[3][4]

Build everyday management records that an independent reviewer can follow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: What does ISO/IEC 42001 cover?
ISO/IEC 42001:2023 covers AI management systems for organizations providing or using AI.[2]
Q2: Is it relevant to users of external AI services?
Yes. ISO’s public description includes organizations using AI systems.[2][3]
Q3: Does ISO issue the certificate?
No. ISO develops standards; external certification bodies perform certification.[4]
Q4: Does certification eliminate answer checks?
We recommend task acceptance checks. Management-system assessment and the accuracy of an individual output are different questions.[2][3]
